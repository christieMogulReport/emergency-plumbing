A full house means a lot of flushing, and "the toilet overflowed" is one of the most common plumbing complaints on social media during holiday weeks. The good news: most overflows can be stopped in under a minute, and most single-toilet clogs can be cleared with the right plunger. This guide draws on toilet manufacturers (American Standard, Kohler), the U.S. EPA's WaterSense program, two public sewer utilities, the Illinois Department of Public Health, This Old House, and Angi cost data, all linked at the bottom.

## Stop the water first (the first 60 seconds)

1. **Don't flush again.** Every flush adds a tank of water to a bowl that can't drain. American Standard says flat out: don't try to clear a clog by flushing over and over.
2. **Take the lid off the tank and push the flapper down.** The flapper is the rubber seal at the bottom of the tank. Pressing it down onto its seat stops tank water from pouring into the bowl. Lay the porcelain lid flat on the floor or a towel so it can't fall and crack.
3. **Lift the float.** The float (a ball on an arm, or a cup that slides up the fill valve) tells the tank to stop refilling. Holding it up stops new water from coming in while you reach for the valve.
4. **Turn the shutoff valve behind or beside the toilet clockwise** until it stops. It's the small valve where the supply hose meets the wall or floor. Don't force a handle that won't move. Old valves can snap.
5. **If that valve won't close or water keeps coming, shut off the main water valve** for the house. This happens more than you'd think: in one [r/Plumbing thread](https://www.reddit.com/r/Plumbing/comments/16j2ubq/my_toilet_has_a_clog_and_continues_to_fill_with/), a homeowner's clogged toilet kept refilling after they "shut it off," and the replies pointed to a worn-out toilet valve plus a bad flapper or fill valve (anecdotal). Our [burst pipe guide](/burst-pipe-what-to-do-first-10-minutes/) covers finding and closing the main shutoff.
6. **Contain it.** Towels around the base and across the doorway, close the door, and keep kids and pets out.

## Is it just this toilet, or the main line?

Before you plunge, check the rest of the house. Run a little water in the bathroom sink and look at the tub.

| What you see | Likely problem | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Only this toilet is stopped; sinks, tub, and other toilets drain fine | A clog in the toilet's built-in trap, usually too much paper or something that shouldn't be flushed | Plunge, then auger (below). Usually fixable tonight. |
| Water rises in the tub or shower when you flush, other drains gurgle, or several fixtures are slow at once | A blockage farther down, in the main drain or sewer line | **Stop using water everywhere.** Don't plunge. See the [sewer backup guide](/sewer-backup-what-to-do-right-now/) and call your city's sewer line and a plumber. |
| Bowl keeps slowly refilling even though nobody flushed | Tank water leaking past the flapper or a fill valve that won't shut off (see "toilet won't stop running" below) | Close the shutoff valve, then clear the clog. |
| A toy, phone, or other solid object went down | Object lodged in the trap | Try a toilet auger to hook it. Don't plunge hard, which can push it deeper. If it won't come out, call a plumber. |

## How to clear a single-toilet clog

**What you need:** rubber gloves, old towels, a bucket and a cup, and a **flange plunger** (the kind with an extra rubber sleeve that folds out of the cup). A flat sink plunger doesn't seal the curved opening at the bottom of a toilet bowl well. This Old House recommends a flange-style or beehive plunger designed for toilets.

1. **Let the water level drop, or bail some out.** If the bowl is near the rim, scoop water into the bucket so you have room to plunge without splashing. You want enough water to cover the rubber cup.
2. **Seat the plunger** over the drain opening with the flange inside it. Tilt it as you put it in so water, not air, fills the cup, and press down slowly to make a seal.
3. **Plunge with firm strokes, and focus on the pull.** This Old House's plumbing expert Richard Trethewey recommends emphasizing the pulling motion: the passage inside a toilet narrows as it goes, so hard pushing can pack the clog in tighter, while pulling breaks it up. This Old House suggests 20 to 30 seconds at a time; American Standard says it can take 5 to 10 minutes of pumping and a few rounds for a stubborn clog.
4. **Test before you flush.** When the water drains away, turn the water back on and flush once (American Standard), keeping your hand ready to push the flapper down if the bowl starts to rise. Or pour in a bucket of water first to test.
5. **If plunging fails, use a toilet (closet) auger.** It's a short cable inside a curved tube with a protective sleeve, made to go through a toilet's trap without scratching the porcelain (This Old House). Crank the cable in until you feel resistance, keep turning to break up or hook the clog, then pull it back toward you. Angi puts a toilet auger at about $10 to $100.

### Don't do this

- **Don't pour chemical drain cleaner into a toilet.** It rarely clears a paper or wipes clog, and if it doesn't work you're left with a bowl of caustic water that you (or the plumber) then have to plunge or snake.
- **Don't use a regular hand drain snake or a wire coat hanger.** Trethewey warns that a standard snake will scratch the porcelain; use a closet auger.
- **Don't keep plunging if other drains are backing up.** If the main line is blocked, the water has nowhere to go and plunging just pushes it out of another drain.

## Toilet won't stop running (or keeps refilling the bowl)

A toilet that runs nonstop is usually not an emergency, but it wastes water and, combined with a clog, keeps refilling a bowl that can't drain. Close the shutoff valve, then check:

- **The flapper chain.** Kohler says the chain should have about one to two links of slack so it doesn't catch under the flapper or on the lever arm.
- **Water spilling into the overflow tube** (the open pipe in the middle of the tank). Kohler says this is often caused by debris lodged in the cap of the fill valve; flushing the debris out of the valve or replacing the fill valve fixes it. If water spills into the overflow but eventually stops, the tank water level just needs adjusting.
- **A worn flapper.** The EPA's WaterSense program says an old or worn flapper can make a toilet flush on its own or silently leak thousands of gallons a year, and recommends checking flappers and replacing them at least every five years. To test: put a few drops of food coloring in the tank; if color shows up in the bowl after 10 minutes without flushing, the flapper leaks (EPA). Flush afterward so it doesn't stain.

Bring the old flapper (or a photo) to the hardware store so the replacement fits (EPA).

## Cleaning up after an overflow

Overflow water from a clogged bowl can carry the same germs as sewage, so treat it that way. The Illinois Department of Public Health's sewage cleanup advice, which we also cover in the [sewer backup guide](/sewer-backup-what-to-do-right-now/):

- **Wear rubber gloves and boots**, and cover any cuts.
- **Throw out what can't be thoroughly cleaned and dried**, such as bath mats and rugs that soaked it up.
- **Wash hard surfaces with soap and warm water, then disinfect** with no more than 1 cup of bleach per gallon of water. **Never mix bleach with ammonia.**
- **Open a window and run a fan**, and wash your hands well afterward.

If water went through the floor and is dripping into a ceiling below, or soaked under vinyl or into subfloor, get it dried out quickly. As our [water heater guide](/water-heater-leaking-what-to-do-tonight/) notes, mold can start within 24 to 48 hours in wet building materials. Take photos before cleanup if you might file an insurance claim.

## Call a plumber tonight, or wait?

**Call tonight** if:

- Water won't stop even with the toilet valve closed, and you can't shut off the main
- Other drains are backing up (main line clog)
- It's the only toilet in the house and plunging and an auger didn't work
- Something solid is stuck and you can't hook it out

**It can usually wait for business hours** if the water is off, the floor is dry, and there's another working toilet. A daytime visit avoids the night, weekend, and holiday premiums described in our [burst pipe guide](/burst-pipe-what-to-do-first-10-minutes/).

### What to ask on the phone

- Is there an after-hours or holiday trip fee, and is it credited toward the job?
- Is it a flat rate to clear a toilet, or hourly?
- If they need to pull the toilet to reach the clog, is resetting it with a new wax ring included?

## Preventing the next one (especially with guests)

- **Flush only toilet paper and human waste.** King County, WA's wastewater program says that's all that belongs in a toilet, and that wipes, even ones labeled "flushable," can build up and cause sewer backups. Winston-Salem, NC says wipes and similar products cause about 20% of the sewer overflows in its system.
- **Put a small trash can with a lid in every bathroom**, so guests aren't tempted to flush wipes, tissues, or hygiene products. American Standard notes that facial tissues, cotton swabs, tampons, and dental floss don't break down in water.
- **Keep a flange plunger in each bathroom**, so nobody has to go looking (or flush "one more time") while the bowl is rising.
- **Test the shutoff valve behind each toilet now.** If it's stiff or doesn't fully stop the water, have it replaced before you need it, and know where your main shutoff is.
- **Same toilet clogging over and over?** That's worth a plumber's visit; there may be a deeper partial blockage. In freezing weather, also see [frozen pipes](/frozen-pipes-how-to-thaw-safely/).
